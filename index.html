> the problem

AI stopped asking for permission.

Most AI products today are built to act, not to check in. A recommendation becomes an action. An action becomes a pattern. A pattern becomes the way something gets decided, quietly, without the person it affects ever seeing the moment a choice was made.

The tools got smarter. The control got smaller, and it kept shrinking until it lived almost entirely inside the companies that trained the model, not the people typing into it. That's not a hypothetical edge case. It's the default architecture of most AI software: confident output, hidden reasoning, and an interface with no lever for a person to pull.

> our belief

Control has to be load-bearing, not decorative.

You can't bolt an off switch onto a finished product and call it safe. If a person's ability to see, question, and reverse an AI's decision isn't part of the architecture from the first line of code, it usually isn't there when it matters, under pressure, at scale, in the one case nobody tested for.

We build the other way around. Every product starts from the human on the other end of it: what they need to see, what they need to be able to stop, and what they need to be able to do without calling support. AI should extend a person's judgment, not stand in for it, and it should be useful to whoever needs it, not only to whoever can afford a data team.
